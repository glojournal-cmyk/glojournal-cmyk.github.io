1. Eukaryotes and prokaryotes

Core distinction

Animal and plant cells are eukaryotic: their genetic material is enclosed in a nucleus. Bacterial cells are prokaryotic: the main DNA loop lies in the cytoplasm without a nucleus. Plasmids are extra small DNA rings and may be present.

Compare fairly

Both types have a membrane, cytoplasm and ribosomes. Typical plant and animal cells have mitochondria; bacteria do not. Both plant and bacterial cells have walls, but bacterial walls are not made of cellulose.

Scale and units

1 mm = 1000 micrometres. 1 micrometre = 1000 nm = 10^-6 m. For a ratio, first convert both sizes to the same unit. A factor of 10 is one order of magnitude; 100 is two orders.

Worked example

30 micrometres compared with 300 nm: 300 nm = 0.3 micrometres. Ratio = 30 / 0.3 = 100. The larger cell is two orders of magnitude wider.

Full-mark method

A comparison should name both sides. Write “plant cells have a nucleus; bacteria have no nucleus”, rather than “plants have a nucleus”. Do not infer relative sizes from a schematic illustration.

2. Animal and plant cells

Structures and functions

Nucleus: genetic material and control of cell activities. Cytoplasm: many chemical reactions. Membrane: controls entry and exit. Mitochondria: aerobic respiration, releasing energy. Ribosomes: protein synthesis.

Plant structures

Cellulose wall strengthens the cell. Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll, which absorbs light for photosynthesis. The permanent vacuole contains cell sap and helps maintain turgor.

Exceptions matter

Underground root hair cells normally lack chloroplasts. Mature mammalian red blood cells lack a nucleus. These exceptions do not change their origin as plant or animal cells.

Full-mark method

Match each structure to its own function. Do not swap membrane with wall, or ribosome with mitochondrion. Say “respiration releases energy”, rather than “mitochondria create energy”.

3. Cell specialisation

Animal adaptations

Sperm: tail for swimming, mitochondria releasing energy for movement, acrosome enzymes helping penetrate the egg. Nerve cell: long axon for transmission over distance and dendrites for connections. Muscle: sliding fibres for contraction, many mitochondria for energy, glycogen as a glucose reserve.

Plant adaptations

Root hair: extension increases surface area for absorption. Xylem: dead hollow vessels carry water and mineral ions; lignin supports the walls. Phloem: sieve plates permit sap flow and companion cells provide energy for sugar transport.

Full-mark method

Use feature + effect + function. Example: “The root hair extension increases surface area, increasing absorption of water and mineral ions.” Just writing “large surface area” does not fully explain its benefit.

School examples

Also revise the red blood cell: haemoglobin carries oxygen, the biconcave shape gives a large surface area relative to volume, and the absence of a nucleus makes room for more haemoglobin.

4. Cell differentiation

Definition

Differentiation is the process by which a cell becomes specialised for a particular function. It acquires appropriate sub-cellular structures, rather than merely becoming larger.

Animal and plant contrast

Most animal cell types differentiate early in development. Many plant cells retain the ability to differentiate throughout life. In mature animals, cell division mainly repairs and replaces cells.

Worked explanation

As an unspecialised cell becomes a muscle cell, it differentiates and develops many mitochondria. These release energy through respiration to enable contraction.

Avoid a common mix-up

Cell division increases cell number. Differentiation changes cell type and structures. They are related processes, but they are not the same. Detailed mitosis stages and stem-cell treatments are outside this stated 4.1.1 test scope.

Full-mark method

For three marks, give the process, a structural change, and the reason the new structure enables a function.

5. Microscopy and required practical 1

Image quality

Magnification is the ratio of image size to actual size. Resolution is the ability to distinguish two close points as separate. Electron microscopes have greater magnification and resolution, revealing finer sub-cellular detail.

Slide preparation

Use a thin onion epidermis layer on a clean slide. Add dilute iodine stain for contrast. Lower the coverslip gently at an angle to reduce air bubbles. For animal cells, use a suitable prepared or teacher-approved slide and stain.

Focus and drawing

Start with the lowest-power objective and use coarse then fine focus. At higher power, use fine focus. Draw only visible structures with clear single lines, no shading, and straight non-crossing labels. Add a title and a magnification scale.

Calculation

M = image size / actual size. Actual size = image size / M. Image size = actual size x M. Use the same length units. Magnification has no length unit.

Worked example

Field width 1.6 mm at x100 becomes 1.6 x 100 / 400 = 0.4 mm at x400. Ten cells across this field gives 0.04 mm = 40 micrometres per cell.

Full-mark method

Write the equation, show conversion and substitution, and give the required unit. A scale bar remains valid when resized with the image, while a stated magnification may not.

6. Culturing microorganisms and required practical 2

Aseptic reasoning

Sterilise agar, dishes and equipment to kill unwanted microorganisms and avoid contamination. Disinfect the bench. Flame the loop and let it cool. Open the lid only slightly and briefly. Secure the lid with a few tape strips rather than sealing all around.

Incubation

For AQA school cultures, use a maximum of 25 degrees C to reduce the risk of growing human pathogens. This reduces risk; it does not guarantee there are no pathogens. Invert plates to prevent condensation dripping onto agar and spreading colonies. Observe cultured plates with lids closed.

RP2 design

Compare antiseptic or antibiotic discs on inoculated agar. Use sterile-water discs as controls. Keep bacterial species, disc size, concentration, agar and incubation conditions comparable. Repeat trials and calculate a mean. Measure clear zones consistently, preferably across two perpendicular diameters when irregular.

Calculations

Population = starting number x 2^(number of doublings). Doublings = elapsed time / division time. Zone area = pi r^2; radius is half the diameter. For a 12 mm diameter, r = 6 mm and area = 3.142 x 36 = 113.1 mm^2.

Interpretation

A larger inhibition zone indicates greater inhibition in those test conditions. Different diffusion rates can influence the result. This alone does not establish which antibiotic works best in a patient.

Full-mark method

Pair every precaution with its reason. Do not use 30 degrees C from the older slide for the AQA school-culture answer.

  2. Assume sufficient nutrients and a suitable temperature / unchanged division time. (1 mark)

Working: 120 / 20 = 6 divisions. 1 x 2^6 = 64 bacteria.

Avoid losing marks: There are six doublings, not six new cells.

67. Describe three aseptic precautions while inoculating an agar plate. 3 marks

  1. Disinfect the bench. (1 mark)
  2. Sterilise the inoculating loop. (1 mark)
  3. Open the lid only slightly and briefly. (1 mark)

68. An initial population of 200 bacteria doubles every 30 minutes. Calculate the population after 3 hours, with working. 3 marks

Show full-mark answer and explanation
  1. Number of doublings = 180 / 30 = 6. (1 mark)
  2. Population = 200 x 2^6. (1 mark)
  3. Final population = 12800 bacteria. (1 mark)

Working: 3 h = 180 min. 180 / 30 = 6. N = 200 x 2^6 = 12800.

Avoid losing marks: Multiply by the starting population; do not assume it is one.

69. One bacterium becomes 128 bacteria with a 30-minute division time. Calculate the time in hours with working. 3 marks

Show full-mark answer and explanation
  1. 128 = 2^7, so seven doublings. (1 mark)
  2. Time = 7 x 30 = 210 minutes. (1 mark)
  3. Time = 3.5 hours. (1 mark)

Working: 128 = 2^7. 7 x 30 = 210 min = 3.5 hours.

Avoid losing marks: Seven doublings are required; divide 210 minutes by 60.

70. A circular clear zone has diameter 12 mm. Calculate its area to 1 decimal place, including working. Use pi = 3.142. 3 marks

Show full-mark answer and explanation
  1. Radius = 6 mm. (1 mark)
  2. Area = pi r squared = 3.142 x 6^2. (1 mark)
  3. Area = 113.1 mm squared. (1 mark)

Working: r = 12 / 2 = 6 mm. A = 3.142 x 6^2 = 113.112 = 113.1 mm^2.

Avoid losing marks: Halve the diameter; area units are mm squared.

71. In an antibiotic-disc experiment, state a suitable control, one controlled variable and how to improve reliability. 3 marks

Show full-mark answer and explanation
  1. Use a disc soaked in sterile water as the control. (1 mark)
  2. Keep antibiotic concentration / disc size / incubation time / bacterial species constant. (1 mark)
  3. Repeat and calculate a mean. (1 mark)

Avoid losing marks: A control treatment and a controlled variable are different things.

72. Under identical conditions antibiotic A gives a 16 mm clear-zone diameter and B gives 10 mm. State the better inhibitor, explain the evidence and give a limitation. 3 marks

Show full-mark answer and explanation
  1. A is the better inhibitor in this test. (1 mark)
  2. A has the larger clear zone / greater inhibited area. (1 mark)
  3. Different diffusion rates can affect zone sizes; this does not alone establish clinical effectiveness. (1 mark)

Avoid losing marks: A larger clear zone supports inhibition under test conditions, not a guaranteed treatment recommendation.
